"Which country is best for me?" has no single answer, but you can answer a better question: what will this country cost me, what can I earn, and what can I do after I graduate? Governments and official study portals publish many of the facts you need. This guide gives you a five-question checklist and applies it to eight destinations, using official sources only.

Last checked: 25 October 2026. Where an official page showed no date, we say so. Tuition, living costs and rules change every year, so use the numbers below to compare, then confirm the ones that matter to you on the linked page.

The five-question checklist

  1. Tuition. Is there any, and who sets it: the government or each university?
  2. Proof of funds. How much must you show for the student visa? This is not what you will spend, but it is the money you must have ready.
  3. Working while you study. How many hours, and do you need permission?
  4. After graduation. Is there a permit to look for work, and how long does it last?
  5. Funding. Which government scholarship exists for international students, and what does it cover?

Day-to-day living costs

  • Germany: DAAD estimates EUR 900 to 1,200 a month depending on the city; a 2023 student survey found students spend on average EUR 876 a month.
  • Japan: privately financed international students spend on average 105,000 yen a month, with housing around 41,000 yen nationwide and 57,000 yen in Tokyo (a 2023 JASSO survey).
  • South Korea: Study in Korea estimates KRW 750,000 to 1,000,000 a month, of which housing is KRW 500,000 to 700,000.

Use these as averages, not promises: your city, your housing and your habits decide your real costs.

Three more worth a look

  • China. Universities set their own tuition, and we found no single official national range. Off-campus work or an internship needs your school's consent and a note on your residence permit. For funding, the Chinese Government Scholarship is run by the China Scholarship Council for the Ministry of Education; see our 2027/28 preparation guide.
  • Türkiye. The government sets lower and upper limits for international-student fees and each university decides the amount. A student residence permit alone does not allow work: you need a separate work permit, and undergraduates' right to work starts after the first year (Study in Türkiye FAQ).
  • Malaysia. International students may work part-time only in semester breaks or holidays longer than 7 days, up to 20 hours a week, with immigration approval. A Graduate Pass allows up to one year after graduation, with conditions.

Government scholarships to look for

  • Germany, DAAD: DAAD scholarships typically pay EUR 992 a month for students and EUR 1,300 for doctoral students, plus a travel allowance, and do not need to be repaid. See our DAAD listing.
  • France, Eiffel: EUR 1,200 a month at master's level, tuition exemption at eligible public institutions, an airfare contribution, insurance and housing support. A French institution has to nominate you. See the Eiffel listing.
  • Ireland: the Government of Ireland International Education Scholarship gives EUR 10,000 plus a full fee waiver for one year of master's or PhD study (60 awards a year; the 2026 call closed on 12 March 2026).
  • South Korea, GKS: covers airfare, Korean-language training, tuition and a monthly allowance. See the GKS listing.
  • Japan, MEXT: tuition exemption, airfare and a monthly stipend; the official page lists several stipend amounts without saying which applies to whom, so read it for your level. See the MEXT listing.
  • Malaysia International Scholarship: tuition plus RM 1,500 a month for master's and PhD study; the 2026/2027 round has closed.

How to turn this into a decision

  1. Write the five answers for your top three countries. One line each. If you cannot find an answer on an official page, that is itself information.
  2. Add the first-year total. Tuition plus the visa proof of funds plus travel and a deposit. This is the cash you need on day one.
  3. Check the funding match. Many scholarships are limited by nationality or level. Use the nationality filter in the scholarship directory before you fall in love with a country.
  4. Think about year three. A job-seeking permit of 12 or 18 months is only worth something if your field has jobs there. Ask the university's career office what share of international graduates find work.
  5. Keep a second choice. Rules and deadlines move; a good plan survives one change.
